# Visual rules

Rules for describing what the viewer sees, for a storyboard or a brief. They hold whether a person shoots the film or a model renders it.

## Shot language

Use the generic vocabulary every crew shares, and nothing more elaborate:

- **Size:** wide, medium, close-up, extreme close-up, insert (a detail such as a switch or a label).
- **Angle:** eye level, low, high, overhead.
- **Movement:** static, pan, tilt, push-in, pull-out, tracking, handheld.
- **Lens feel, when it matters:** shallow focus, deep focus.

One line per shot: size, movement, subject, action. "Medium, slow push-in, the lamp on the desk, the arm folds flat." That is a complete shot description.

## The product stays put

When a model renders the film, give the movement to the camera and to human hands, and leave the product where it is. A rendered product that has to turn, tip or travel is the shot most likely to come back wrong; a camera that travels past a product that stays put almost always comes back right. A filmed turntable or a real unboxing is a different matter; this rule is about what is generated.

## A single move, run to its end

Decide the one thing the camera does in a shot before writing the line, and let it finish before the cut. A shot that changes its mind halfway looks like a camera searching for something. Anything the camera does after that is the next shot, with its own line in the storyboard.

## Copy is composited, never rendered

Lettering is not something a generator draws well enough to keep: letters drift, spellings break, and the wordmark comes out wrong. Describe an empty area where the copy will sit, and add the words afterwards in an editor. The same goes for prices, dates and any legal line.

## The wordmark stays small

A logo is a signature, not a headline. It sits in a corner or at the end, small enough to be found and not so large that it competes with the product. The product's benefit is the thing on screen; the brand name is what the viewer takes away after.

## Describe the state you want, not the flaw you fear

"Even, soft light from a window on the left" works. "No harsh shadows, not blurry, not distorted" does not: a negative description gives the maker nothing to aim at and often plants the flaw it names. Say what is there, how it is lit, what it is made of, what the hands are doing.

## Light, surfaces, hands

- Say where the light comes from: a window, a lamp, overhead daylight, evening. Where the light comes from explains the mood better than an adjective does.
- Name the surface the product sits on: wood, linen, marble, a laptop keyboard. Surfaces give scale and setting.
- Put a hand in the frame when scale or use matters. A hand tells the viewer how big the thing is and that it is real.

## Storyboard format

One row per shot: number, time (from the beat map), size and movement, what is seen, what is heard. Keep it to one page. A storyboard that needs scrolling has too many shots for its length.

## Example, three shots, invented brand

Ashbeam, a desk lamp that folds flat and clips to the monitor.

| Shot | Time | Shot language | Seen | Heard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Close-up, static, low angle | A monitor's top edge; a hand clips the lamp on in one motion | A click |
| 2 | 2 to 6 s | Medium, slow pull-out | The whole desk lights evenly; the keyboard has no shadow | "Light where you type, not where you look." |
| 3 | 6 to 9 s | Insert, static | The hand folds the arm flat against the monitor | "And gone when you are done." |
