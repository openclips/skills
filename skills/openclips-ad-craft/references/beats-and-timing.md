# Beats and timing

A film has a length before it has words. Fix the length, divide it into beats, give each beat one action, then fit the words to the beats. Writing the words first produces scripts that do not fit and films that rush.

## The word budget

Spoken delivery at a natural pace runs about two and a half to three words a second, and the film does not talk from its first frame to its last. A 15-second film holds roughly thirty-five to forty words, not sixty. Count the words of every script and say the count. When the count is over budget, cut lines, not pauses.

A translation is rarely the same length as the English. When a film is translated, retime it rather than trusting the English count.

## Room at both ends

Leave the first half-second and the last second nearly empty of speech. The opening needs a beat for the eye to land; the ending needs a beat for the last line to settle and the call to action to be read. A script that starts talking at zero and stops at the final frame feels cut off at both ends.

## One action per beat

A beat is the smallest unit the viewer experiences as one thing: a look, a gesture, a reveal, a line. Two actions in one beat are two beats. If a beat needs to do two things, it is the wrong length.

## Beat maps by length

Each map names the job of each beat and its rough share of the time. Adjust the shares to the product, not the other way round.

### 10 seconds

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Hook: the tension, the result, or the demonstration begins |
| 2 | 2 to 6 s | What it does, shown |
| 3 | 6 to 8 s | The specific fact that makes it credible |
| 4 | 8 to 10 s | The name and one instruction, with room to land |

### 15 seconds

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Hook |
| 2 | 2 to 5 s | The problem, recognised |
| 3 | 5 to 10 s | The one thing it does, shown, with the fact |
| 4 | 10 to 13 s | The result or the second fact |
| 5 | 13 to 15 s | Name and call to action |

### 20 seconds

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Hook |
| 2 | 2 to 6 s | Problem in the viewer's words |
| 3 | 6 to 11 s | Demonstration |
| 4 | 11 to 15 s | Proof: a fact, a comparison, a detail up close |
| 5 | 15 to 18 s | The result in daily life |
| 6 | 18 to 20 s | Name and call to action |

### 30 seconds

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Hook |
| 2 | 2 to 7 s | Problem, with a moment of recognition |
| 3 | 7 to 13 s | Demonstration, the main action |
| 4 | 13 to 19 s | Second use or second fact |
| 5 | 19 to 24 s | Objection, answered |
| 6 | 24 to 28 s | Result, the life after |
| 7 | 28 to 30 s | Name and call to action |

### 40 seconds

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | Hook |
| 2 | 2 to 8 s | The situation before |
| 3 | 8 to 15 s | Demonstration |
| 4 | 15 to 22 s | How it works, one level deeper |
| 5 | 22 to 29 s | Proof and a detail up close |
| 6 | 29 to 35 s | Objection answered, or a second person's use |
| 7 | 35 to 38 s | Result |
| 8 | 38 to 40 s | Name and call to action |

## Writing the script against the map

Write the beat number, the time, what is seen, then the words. Keep the words plain and in the speaker's own register. Read it aloud with a timer once; if a beat runs over, cut the words in that beat, never squeeze the next one.

## Example, 15 seconds, invented brand

Tessamund, a face cream sold on one fact: it absorbs in under a minute.

| Beat | Time | Seen | Said |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 2 s | A hand, a dab of cream, a wristwatch in frame | "Watch the second hand." |
| 2 | 2 to 5 s | The cream spread on the back of the hand | "An hour later, still tacky?" |
| 3 | 5 to 10 s | Time-lapse of the hand; the sheen disappears | "This one is gone in under a minute. No film, no wait." |
| 4 | 10 to 13 s | The person picks up a phone with the same hand, no smear | "Which means you can actually touch things." |
| 5 | 13 to 15 s | The jar, held still; the name on the frame | "Tessamund." |

Twenty-nine words, and the last second is the name on screen with nothing spoken over it.
