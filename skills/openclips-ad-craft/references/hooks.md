# Hooks

The opening moment is most of the ad. Scrolling viewers give a video about two seconds, and the product has usually not been named by then. So the hook is not the first line of the pitch; it is the reason the pitch gets heard at all.

## What a hook is

One idea, delivered in the first breath, that makes the next second worth watching. It can be a line spoken to camera, a caption over the first frame, a visual event, or a sound. The best hooks do two of those at once: a caption and an action that agree.

## Angles that work

Pick the angle from the product's own facts. A hook that could open any ad is not a hook.

- **The tension:** name the problem in the viewer's words, not the brand's. "Three years typing in shadow."
- **The after, first:** open on what it looks like once the problem is gone, before the how. "This is what my desk looks like with the lamp put away."
- **The odd fact:** one specific, surprising number or property. "Lighter than its own charging cable."
- **The reversal:** start with the thing people expect and turn it. "I bought a desk lamp because I wanted more desk."
- **The demonstration:** the product does the one thing, on camera, with no words. A lamp folding flat in one motion is its own hook.
- **The quiet confession:** the speaker owns a small failure of their own before the product appears. "I put this off for two years."
- **The question the viewer already has:** "Does it wobble when the monitor moves?" Then answer it.
- **The before, then the cut:** an unglamorous frame of the problem, and the cut to the answer lands inside the first two seconds; the recognition and the change happen almost together.

## Openings to avoid

- Greetings and introductions. A wave and a name spend the opening on the speaker instead of the viewer.
- The logo, the wordmark or the packaging as the first frame. The product's benefit goes first; the brand name comes when the opening is over, and the beat map puts it last.
- Adjectives without a noun to hang on: "amazing", "game-changing", "premium". They describe the writer's hopes, not the product.
- A question with an obvious yes. "Do you want a better desk?" spends the opening on nothing.
- Slow establishing shots. If the first two seconds could be trimmed without losing meaning, they are not the opening.
- Music-only intros. Assume the feed is silent: an opening that needs its audio has no opening.

## Writing a set

Write five to eight, each on its own line, each from a different angle. Beat one is about two seconds with a half-second of room at the front, so a spoken hook is four or five words; a longer line is a caption over the first frame, or spills into beat two on purpose, and the set says which. Name the angle beside each so the user can choose by intent rather than by taste. Then say which one the facts support best, and why in one sentence.

## Example set, invented brand

For Murmurlist, a notes app that turns a voice memo into a to-do list:

1. Tension, spoken: "Forty memos I never opened."
2. The after, first, caption: "My to-do list. I never typed it."
3. Demonstration, no words: a thumb holds the record button; a list appears line by line as the person talks.
4. Reversal, caption: "I stopped taking notes. My notes got better."
5. The viewer's question, spoken: "What if I ramble?" Then the list, tidy anyway.
6. Odd fact, caption: "Eleven seconds of talking. Six tasks."
